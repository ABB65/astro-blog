## Onboarding Is a Product, Not an Event

Most companies treat onboarding as a checklist: accounts created, laptop shipped, welcome email sent. For remote teams, that's not enough. Without a physical office to absorb context by osmosis, the first thirty days need to be deliberately designed.

## Where Remote Onboarding Breaks Down

The failure points are rarely about missing tools. They're about ambiguity:

1. **No single owner.** When onboarding is "everyone's job," it becomes no one's job, and new hires are left piecing context together from scattered Slack threads.
2. **Too much, too fast.** Dumping every internal doc on day one overwhelms rather than informs. New hires need a sequence, not an archive.
3. **No feedback loop.** Without regular check-ins in the first few weeks, small confusions compound into disengagement.

## What Works Instead

Teams that onboard well remotely tend to share a few practices:

- A **named onboarding buddy** who isn't the manager, giving new hires a low-stakes person to ask "dumb questions."
- A **structured first-week plan** with concrete, small tasks that produce visible output by day three or four.
- **Written context over meetings** — async documentation that can be revisited, rather than a single orientation call that's forgotten by lunch.

## Measuring Success

The right metric isn't "did they complete the checklist," but "do they feel confident contributing independently by week four." That's a harder thing to track, but it's the thing that actually predicts retention.

Good onboarding is quiet infrastructure — invisible when it works, and painfully obvious when it doesn't.
