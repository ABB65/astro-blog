## Novelty Has a Cost

Every new framework promises to solve the problems of the last one. Some do. But every adoption also carries a hidden cost: fewer people who've debugged it at 2am, fewer Stack Overflow answers, fewer battle-tested patterns to copy.

"Boring technology" isn't a rejection of progress. It's a recognition that a team only has so much innovation budget, and it should be spent where it matters most for the product — not on the plumbing underneath it.

## Where to Spend the Budget

A useful mental model: pick one or two areas where being on the cutting edge actually differentiates your product, and be deliberately conservative everywhere else.

- If your product's edge is real-time collaboration, invest there — and use a boring, proven database underneath.
- If your differentiation is content velocity, invest in your content pipeline — and keep your deployment stack unremarkable.

## The Signs of Over-Investment in Novelty

A few patterns are worth watching for:

- Migrating to a new tool before the old one has caused an actual, named problem.
- Choosing a framework because of community excitement rather than fit for the team's skill set.
- Rewriting working systems because they're "not fun to work in anymore."

## Boring Doesn't Mean Stagnant

Choosing proven technology doesn't mean freezing in place. It means upgrading deliberately, on your own schedule, rather than chasing every new release. The goal isn't to avoid change — it's to make change a choice instead of a reaction.

Teams that last aren't the ones with the most exciting stack. They're the ones who spent their creativity on the product their users actually feel.
